STRATEGY & CONCLUSION — PRESENTATION SCRIPT
(approx. 4 minutes at a normal speaking pace)

------------------------------------------------------------
PART 1 — THE STRATEGY  (~1 min 45 sec)
------------------------------------------------------------

So here is the actual trading strategy, and I'll keep this in
plain terms.

The "label" our model is trying to predict is something called the
Variance Risk Premium, or VRP. Think of it as a residual: it is
the difference between how much the stock actually moves tomorrow
and how much the options market today is *implying* it will move.
If our model says that gap is going to be positive, the market is
under-pricing volatility, and the right move is to *buy* a
straddle — that is the option contract that pays off whenever the
stock moves a lot in either direction. If our model says the gap
will be negative, we *sell* the straddle, because the market is
over-paying for movement that won't happen.

To avoid trading on noise, we only take the trade when the
predicted VRP is large enough — specifically, when it crosses a
threshold. And here is the part I want to emphasize for a CS
audience: that threshold is not hand-picked. It is chosen by a
nested walk-forward search — basically a rolling cross-validation
where we re-fit on an expanding training window, pick the
threshold that maximized the Sharpe ratio in-sample, and then
apply it forward to genuinely unseen data. So nothing in our
reported results has peeked at the test set.

On the Strategy page you'll see the JETS cumulative P&L curve
climbing steadily through the entire out-of-sample window —
that's this rule firing day after day on data the model never
saw during training.

PART 2 — THE CONCLUSION  (~2 min 15 sec)
------------------------------------------------------------

So, did our hypotheses hold up?

Recall we made three claims on top of the HAR baseline. First,
that implied volatility — the option market's forward view —
would add predictive power. Second, that oil volatility would
leak into airline vol through the jet-fuel cost channel. And
third, our central claim, that the Texas Oil Stock Index — oil-
sector equity sentiment — would add signal beyond what those
price-based features already capture.

Here is the scorecard. **IV: partially supported.** **OVX:
not supported.** **TOSI: weakly supported.** Before I unpack
each one, a quick note on *why we ran four model families* —
OLS, Ridge, Random Forest, and XGBoost — instead of just picking
one. Honestly, we didn't know in advance which would win, and
the comparison itself turned out to be a finding. **Linear OLS
beats every more flexible model on average RMSE for the macro-
feature specifications.** The only place a tree model wins is
LUV under bare HAR-RV, where Random Forest captures a non-linear
interaction between the daily, weekly, and monthly RV components.
The takeaway for an ML audience: in this domain, *feature
engineering beat model complexity.* The signal is essentially
linear once you have the right inputs.

That also explains why the HAR baseline itself is so hard to
beat. Our full model improves root mean squared error by less
than one percent. Volatility is mostly its own autocorrelated
past, and any extra feature has to fight for marginal lift.

On implied volatility: it carries real forward-looking
information, but only crosses statistical significance for the
JETS ETF, where the Diebold-Mariano test gives a p-value around
0.001. For the individual airlines the lift is directionally
positive but doesn't clear the bar. The forward-looking-options
story holds, but only at the sector-aggregate level.

On oil volatility: the jet-fuel cost story does *not* hold in the
forecasting sense. OVX co-moves with airline vol contemporaneously
— you can see it in the correlation heatmap — but it does not
*lead* next-day RV once HAR already knows recent realized vol.

And on TOSI, our central hypothesis, the verdict is genuinely
mixed. *No* ticker shows TOSI as statistically significant — the
best p-value is about 0.10, which in ML terms is "interesting but
not conclusive." However — and this matters — the full
specification *including* TOSI produces the lowest average RMSE
of any combination we tried, and it is selected as the per-ticker
best model for three of our four names. So TOSI is not a silver
bullet, but it is a useful weak signal in an ensemble.

Where this all becomes economically real is JETS. Our full-spec
OLS strategy on JETS achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of
about 2.7 on threshold-filtered straddles. For context, a Sharpe
above 2 is the kind of number hedge funds build entire desks
around. So even though the statistical lift is small, the
economic lift, when amplified through a disciplined trading rule,
is genuinely tradeable.

Our overall takeaway: simple models win most of the time,
forward-looking option prices add real but ticker-specific value,
and a noisy sentiment feature like TOSI is worth keeping in the
ensemble even when it can't win on its own.

Thank you.

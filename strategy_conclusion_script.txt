PART 1 — THE STRATEGY  (~1 min 45 sec)
------------------------------------------------------------

Now that we’ve seen what our model predicts, the natural next
question is:

Can those predictions actually translate into a profitable trading
strategy?

To answer that, we built a rule around a concept called the
Variance Risk Premium, or VRP. In simple terms, the VRP is the gap
between how much a stock actually moves tomorrow and how much the
options market today expects it to move. It’s the market’s
mispricing of volatility — and it’s exactly what our model is
trying to forecast.

The logic of the strategy is straightforward. If our model says
that gap will be positive, the market is under-pricing volatility,
so we buy a straddle — an options position that profits whenever
the stock makes a large move in either direction. If the model
says the gap will be negative, we do the opposite and sell the
straddle, because the market is paying too much for movement that
isn’t going to happen.

To keep us from trading on noise, we only act when the predicted
VRP crosses a threshold. And here’s the important part: that
threshold isn’t hand-picked. It’s chosen through a nested walk-
forward search — a rolling cross-validation that retrains on an
expanding window, selects the threshold that maximized Sharpe in-
sample, and then applies it forward to completely unseen data.
Nothing in our reported results has peeked at the test set.

On the Strategy page, you’ll see the JETS cumulative P&L climbing
steadily through the entire out-of-sample window — that’s this
rule firing day after day on data the model had never seen.

PART 2 — THE CONCLUSION  (~2 min 15 sec)
------------------------------------------------------------

So, coming back to where we started — did oil volatility and oil
sentiment actually help us predict airline stock volatility?

Recall our three hypotheses on top of the HAR baseline. First,
that implied volatility — the option market’s forward-looking view
— would add predictive power. Second, that oil volatility would
leak into airline volatility through the jet-fuel cost channel.
And third: that TOSI — text oil sentiment indicator — would add signal beyond what
price-based features already capture.

Here’s the scorecard.

Implied volatility: partially supported.
Oil volatility (OVX): not supported.
TOSI: weakly supported.

Before unpacking each one, a quick note on why we ran four model
families — OLS, Ridge, Random Forest, and XGBoost — instead of
just picking one. Honestly, we didn’t know in advance which would
win, and the comparison itself became a finding:

Linear OLS beat every more flexible model on average RMSE for the
macro-feature specifications.

In this domain, feature engineering beat model
complexity. The signal is essentially linear once you have the
right inputs.

That also explains why the HAR baseline itself is so hard to beat.
Our full model improves RMSE by less than one percent. Volatility
is mostly its own autocorrelated past, and every extra feature has
to fight for marginal lift.

On implied volatility: it carries real forward-looking
information, but it only crosses statistical significance for the
JETS ETF, with a Diebold-Mariano p-value around 0.001. For the
individual airlines, the lift is directionally positive but
doesn’t clear the bar. The forward-looking-options story holds —
but only at the sector-aggregate level.

On oil volatility: the jet-fuel cost story does not hold in the
forecasting sense. OVX co-moves with airline volatility
contemporaneously — you can see it in the correlation heatmap —
but it does not lead next-day volatility once HAR already knows
the recent realized vol. What this means is that the market is 
already pricing in the oil volatility information within the 
airline volatility, so adding the OVX volatility doesn't add predictive
power.

And on TOSI — our central hypothesis — the verdict is genuinely
mixed. No ticker shows TOSI as statistically significant on its
own; the best p-value is about 0.10, which in ML terms is
“interesting, but not conclusive.” However — and this matters —
the full specification including TOSI produces the lowest average
RMSE of any combination we tried, and it is selected as the per-
ticker best model for three of our four names. So TOSI isn’t a
silver bullet, but it’s a useful weak signal inside an ensemble.

Where this all becomes economically real is JETS. Our full-spec
OLS strategy on JETS achieves an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of
about 2.7 on threshold-filtered straddles. For context, a Sharpe
above 2 is the kind of number hedge funds build entire desks
around. So even though the statistical lift is small, the economic
lift — once amplified through a disciplined trading rule — is
genuinely tradeable. That being said, our trading simulation is effectively
a upper limit, as in the real world there would be transaction costs
that reduce our returns and therefore sharpe ratio.

So what’s the big picture?
                                                                                                                                      
Three things stand out. First, once the simple linear model has the right features,
it is tough to beat. Second, forward-looking option prices genuinely add value, 
but that value is ticker-specific and really shines at the sector
level with JETS. And third, even a noisy sentiment signal like TOSI 
earns its place in the ensemble — it doesn’t win on its own, but
it consistently helps the models that do.

In short: better features beat fancier models, and weak signals, combined carefully, can still move the needle.
Combining oil price volatility and text oil sentiment indicator marginally improve
forecasting of airline stock volatility.

Thank you.
